Below is a **visual catalog of 10 important olive cultivars used primarily for oil production**. For olives, “taste” is most useful when describing the **oil**, not the raw fruit, because fresh olives are intensely bitter before processing. Also, skin color changes dramatically with ripeness, so I give the typical progression rather than a single fixed color. ([International Olive Council][1])

### 1. Arbequina — Spain

**Skin:** Green → reddish-purple → dark purple/black
**Flesh:** Pale greenish-cream, becoming beige/tan as ripe
**Oil taste:** Very fruity, smooth and aromatic, with little bitterness or pungency. Typical impressions include ripe fruit, apple, almond and occasionally banana-like notes. ([International Olive Council][2])

**Flavor style:** Mild / fruity / sweet
**Bitterness:** ★☆☆☆☆
**Pepperiness:** ★☆☆☆☆
**Intensity:** ★★☆☆☆

A good choice for people who prefer **gentle, approachable olive oils**.

---

### 2. Picual — Spain

**Skin:** Green → wine-red → deep purple-black
**Flesh:** Cream-green when immature; pale beige when fully ripe
**Oil taste:** Distinctly green and structured, with olive leaf, green almond and artichoke notes. Usually moderately bitter and peppery. ([International Olive Council][3])

**Flavor style:** Green / herbal / robust
**Bitterness:** ★★★★☆
**Pepperiness:** ★★★★☆
**Intensity:** ★★★★☆

Picual is particularly valued when a **strong, persistent EVOO** is desired.

---

### 3. Koroneiki — Greece

**Skin:** Bright green → violet → nearly black
**Flesh:** Pale green to cream/tan
**Oil taste:** Highly fruity with pronounced **herbal-green character**, normally accompanied by medium bitterness and pungency. ([UC Agriculture and Natural Resources][4])

**Flavor style:** Herbaceous / fresh / peppery
**Bitterness:** ★★★☆☆
**Pepperiness:** ★★★★☆
**Intensity:** ★★★★☆

Koroneiki fruit is exceptionally small, but the cultivar has an outstanding reputation for oil quality.

---

### 4. Frantoio — Italy

**Skin:** Green → reddish-purple → purple-black
**Flesh:** Pale cream-green to tan
**Oil taste:** Fresh-cut grass, green pepper, tomato, green almond and often artichoke. Bitterness and pungency are usually medium and well balanced. ([International Olive Council][3])

**Flavor style:** Grassy / artichoke / almond
**Bitterness:** ★★★☆☆
**Pepperiness:** ★★★☆☆
**Intensity:** ★★★★☆

Frantoio is one of the classic cultivars behind the characteristic **Tuscan-style EVOO profile**.

---

### 5. Coratina — Italy

**Skin:** Green → reddish-purple → dark violet/black
**Flesh:** Pale greenish cream → beige
**Oil taste:** Very powerful. Green grass, olive leaf, green almond and green banana peel are typical, followed by **high bitterness and an intense, persistent peppery sensation**. ([International Olive Council][3])

**Flavor style:** Very robust / green / pungent
**Bitterness:** ★★★★★
**Pepperiness:** ★★★★★
**Intensity:** ★★★★★

Among these ten cultivars, Coratina is one of the clearest choices for someone who actively wants **strong bitterness, polyphenol-driven pungency and structure**.

---

### 6. Leccino — Italy

**Skin:** Green → reddish-purple → dark purple/black
**Flesh:** Cream to beige
**Oil taste:** Softer and rounder than Frantoio or Coratina. Generally balanced between sweet, bitter and peppery sensations, with ripe-fruit and sweet-almond aromas. ([Frantoio Malavalle][5])

**Flavor style:** Delicate / ripe fruit / almond
**Bitterness:** ★★☆☆☆
**Pepperiness:** ★★☆☆☆
**Intensity:** ★★☆☆☆

Leccino is often useful in blends because it **softens more aggressive cultivars without becoming flavorless**.

---

### 7. Arbosana — Spain

**Skin:** Green → pale yellow → pinkish-purple → dark purple
**Flesh:** Pale green/cream → beige
**Oil taste:** Fruity and aromatic, but typically more bitter, pungent and green than Arbequina. Artichoke, aromatic herbs, green fruit and sometimes pistachio-like notes may appear. ([UCANR][6])

**Flavor style:** Fruity / green / moderately peppery
**Bitterness:** ★★★☆☆
**Pepperiness:** ★★★☆☆
**Intensity:** ★★★☆☆

Think of Arbosana as roughly **a more structured, greener relative of Arbequina**.

---

### 8. Hojiblanca — Spain

**Skin:** Pale green → yellow-green → red-violet → purple-black
**Flesh:** Cream to beige
**Oil taste:** Generally fruity and elegant, often showing freshly cut grass, herbs, green almond and sometimes artichoke. Moderate bitterness is often followed by a more noticeable peppery finish.

**Flavor style:** Herbal / almond / balanced
**Bitterness:** ★★★☆☆
**Pepperiness:** ★★★☆☆
**Intensity:** ★★★☆☆

Hojiblanca is technically **dual-purpose**, but its oil is highly regarded despite relatively modest oil content. The IOC specifically describes it as a dual-purpose cultivar whose oil quality is highly valued. ([International Olive Council][7])

---

### 9. Picholine — France

**Skin:** Light green → yellow-green → reddish-purple → black
**Flesh:** Pale cream to beige
**Oil taste:** Fresh and aromatic, usually green and herbal with nutty/almond nuances and a peppery finish.

**Flavor style:** Herbal / nutty / fresh
**Bitterness:** ★★★☆☆
**Pepperiness:** ★★★☆☆
**Intensity:** ★★★☆☆

Picholine is another **dual-purpose cultivar**, but it is capable of producing distinctive aromatic oil as well as excellent green table olives.

---

### 10. Mission — United States / California

**Skin:** Green → purple-red → very dark purple/black
**Flesh:** Cream-green → tan when ripe
**Oil taste:** Generally medium-to-robust, fruity and somewhat nutty, with noticeable bitterness and pungency when harvested early. UC Davis specifically notes that Mission can contribute substantial robustness to blends. ([UC Davis][8])

**Flavor style:** Fruity / nutty / robust
**Bitterness:** ★★★☆☆
**Pepperiness:** ★★★☆☆
**Intensity:** ★★★★☆

Mission is also a dual-purpose olive, but historically it has been an important oil cultivar in California.

---

One important point when comparing olive oils: **cultivar is only part of the flavor equation**. Harvesting the same cultivar while the fruit is mostly green generally produces a greener, more bitter and more pungent oil; allowing it to ripen further generally shifts the oil toward softer, sweeter and riper-fruit aromas. The IOC therefore classifies olive-oil sensory profiles broadly from **intense green fruitiness through mild green fruitiness to ripe fruitiness**. ([International Olive Council][9])

If I were arranging these ten from **mildest → strongest**, a useful approximate sequence would be:

**Arbequina → Leccino → Arbosana → Hojiblanca → Picholine → Frantoio → Koroneiki → Mission → Picual → Coratina.**

That ordering is particularly useful if your next goal is to decide **which cultivars to plant for producing your own olive oil**.
